Bumrungrad vs Samitivej vs Bangkok Hospital: which one to pick for your situation

Bangkok's three big private hospitals. Different crowds, different specialties, different price tags.

Marc· Published 29 October 2023· Updated 12 August 2025· 10 min read

The night I had to choose in a hurry

In March 2021, I woke up at 3 a.m. with stomach pain so bad it folded me in half. The kind of thing where you don't think, you just call a Grab and go. Except that in the Grab, between waves of nausea, I still had time to ask myself: where am I going?

I was living in Phrom Phong back then. Bumrungrad was ten minutes away, Samitivej Sukhumvit about seven. Bangkok Hospital a bit further down on Soi 47. I picked Samitivej because it was closest. Appendicitis. Operated on that same morning. Bill: 187,000 baht. My insurance covered 80%.

Since then, I've spent plenty of time in all three — for myself, for friends I had to take in, for annual check-ups. And I can tell you they are not the same. Not because one is "better" than the others in some absolute sense, but because each one has its own DNA, its target clientele, and its strengths. Here's what seven years have taught me.

Bumrungrad: the international machine

Bumrungrad is the behemoth. Over a million patients a year, including 520,000 foreigners from 190 countries. The place looks more like an international airport than a hospital. Marble lobby, Starbucks on the ground floor, interpreters in Arabic, Japanese, Mandarin, Burmese. When you walk in, you almost wonder whether you've entered the wrong building.

Bumrungrad's strong suit is cardiology, oncology, and orthopedic surgery. Their cardiac center is probably the best in Southeast Asia. I went along with a 58-year-old friend for a triple bypass in 2023. The surgical team was flawless, the post-op follow-up highly structured. Total bill for 8 days of hospitalization: roughly 1.2 million baht. Expensive? Yes. But that's 60% cheaper than the same procedure in Singapore and a quarter of the price in the US.

The flip side: the factory feel. You're a number. Consultations last 10–15 minutes. The doctors are skilled but rushed. And if you come in for something "routine" — an ear infection, a sprain, a blood panel — you'll wait, pay top baht, and feel like you've been through a conveyor belt.

Practical tip: At Bumrungrad, always ask for a written estimate before any procedure. Their "packages" (check-ups, planned surgery) are often 20 to 30% cheaper than itemized billing. And book through the app — walking into the ER on a Saturday means at least a 2-hour wait.

Samitivej: the expat family favorite

Samitivej is my everyday hospital. It's where I go for GP visits, minor issues, annual check-ups. There are several campuses — Sukhumvit, Srinakarin, Thonburi — but the Sukhumvit branch (Soi 49) is home base for the local expat crowd.

The vibe is radically different from Bumrungrad. Smaller, calmer, more "human." The doctors take their time. My GP, Dr. Somchai (I've been seeing him for five years), asks about my lifestyle, reminds me I drink too much coffee, asks how my cat is doing. It sounds silly but it makes all the difference.

Their pediatrics department is excellent. That's actually why most Japanese and Korean families in the neighborhood go there — the integrated Japanese Medical Center is a real asset. Their maternity ward also has a great reputation. Two colleagues of mine gave birth there, and both were very happy with the experience.

On the other hand, for serious cases — cancer, cardiac surgery, neurosurgery — Samitivej isn't on the same level as Bumrungrad or Bangkok Hospital. They know it, and they don't hesitate to refer patients elsewhere, which is actually a sign of honesty.

Price-wise, it's 10 to 20% cheaper than Bumrungrad for comparable services in general medicine and common specialties. A GP consultation runs around 800–1,200 baht (excluding medication and tests).

Bangkok Hospital: the network and the niche specialties

Bangkok Hospital is the flagship of the BDMS group, the largest private hospital network in Thailand. And you can tell. The main hospital on Soi Soonvijai is massive — 17 floors, dozens of specialized centers. It's also the most "Thai" of the three in terms of clientele: lots of well-off local patients, politicians, high-ranking military officers.

Their strength lies in neurology and neurosurgery (the Bangkok International Neuroscience Center is a regional reference), transplant medicine, and robotic surgery. They also have an excellent trauma center — if you have a serious motorbike accident in Bangkok, that's probably where you want to end up.

The other major advantage: the BDMS network. If you travel around Thailand — Phuket, Chiang Mai, Samui, Hua Hin — there are hospitals from the same group everywhere. Your medical records follow you. That's a real comfort when you live here year-round.

The downside: English communication can be less smooth than at Bumrungrad. Not an issue with the specialists (many of whom studied abroad), but with nurses and admin staff, you sometimes have to work for it. And the buildings, despite recent renovations, are showing their age in places.

The insurance question

All three hospitals accept most international insurance plans — Cigna, Allianz Worldwide, Bupa, AXA, April International. But the devil is in the details.

Bumrungrad has the most "inflated" billing of the three. Not in a fraudulent way, but they tend to pile on extra tests and specialist referrals. My insurer (Cigna) once called me to check whether I really needed three specialist consultations for back pain. The answer was no — Bumrungrad's system had automatically shuffled me from one department to the next.

Samitivej and Bangkok Hospital are a bit more reasonable on that front. But regardless of where you go, if you're on direct billing, always ask for an itemized invoice. Some charges are negotiable, especially medication: you can request generics instead of brand-name drugs.

Insurance tip: If your plan has a moderate annual cap (under 2 million baht), stick with Samitivej for routine care. Save Bumrungrad or Bangkok Hospital for situations where you genuinely need their specific expertise. That way you won't burn through your cap on everyday visits.

My personal decision tree

After seven years, here's how I operate:

General medicine, regular follow-up, minor emergency: Samitivej Sukhumvit. I know the doctors, it's quick, it's personal, and the prices are reasonable.

Heart problem, suspected cancer, major orthopedic surgery: Bumrungrad. It's the machine, but for these cases that's exactly what you need — teams who do this fifty times a week.

Accident, trauma, neurological issue: Bangkok Hospital. Their trauma center is battle-tested, and the neuro center has no equivalent at the other two.

Childbirth, pediatrics: Samitivej, no contest.

Traveling upcountry and need continuity of care: Bangkok Hospital (BDMS network).

What I wish someone had told me

When I landed in Bangkok in 2018, I thought "best hospital" was a simple question with a single answer. It's not. All three are JCI-accredited, all equipped with cutting-edge gear, all staffed by doctors who trained at top universities around the world. The real question isn't "which one is the best" but "which one is the best for my specific situation, my budget, and my lifestyle."

I also wish someone had told me there's no need to be loyal to one hospital out of principle. There's absolutely no shame in doing your annual check-up at Samitivej and getting your knee surgery at Bumrungrad. It's actually the smartest strategy.

And above all: I wish someone had told me to get proper health insurance before I needed it. Because 187,000 baht for an appendectomy is manageable. But a stay in intensive care or cardiac surgery can run into the millions. And at that point, the question of which hospital is 15% cheaper than the other becomes pretty much beside the point.
