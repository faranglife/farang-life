Riding a Scooter in Bangkok: A Practical Guide to Not Ending Up in the ER

How to rent, where to rent, which areas to avoid, and what the police actually check. The no-BS guide.

Marc· Published 18 May 2024· Updated January 2025· 10 min read

In March 2018, three months after I landed in Bangkok, I rented a Honda Click 125 from a small shop on Ramkhamhaeng. The guy handed me the key, said "careful" with a smile, and that was it. No contract, no photos of the scooter, no formal deposit. I stalled twice before making it out of the parking lot, and twenty minutes later I was riding down Ratchadaphisek in rush hour, wedged between a bus that didn't believe in brakes and a sidecar loaded with pork skewers. I nearly died three times that day. It was also the day I understood that riding in Bangkok has absolutely nothing to do with what you're used to back in Europe — and that nobody actually explains the rules of the game before you hop on.

Seven years and zero serious accidents later (knock on wood, and on my building's spirit house), here's the guide I wish I'd had back then.

Renting a scooter: your options and the pitfalls

There are essentially three ways to rent a scooter in Bangkok:

Tourist rental shops (Khao San Road, Sukhumvit, Silom): the easiest option, the most expensive, and often the riskiest from a legal standpoint. They'll ask for your passport as a deposit — never hand it over. A photocopy is enough at any reputable place. Expect to pay 250–350 baht per day for a basic automatic scooter.

Local rental shops (outside tourist zones): cheaper, often more straightforward, but you'll need at least some basic Thai or a Thai-speaking friend. I've got a guy in Lat Phrao who rents me a Honda PCX 160 for 3,500 baht a month. He does a proper contract in Thai, takes a 5,000 baht cash deposit, and his scooters are actually maintained.

Long-term rental / leasing: if you're staying more than six months, this is the smart play. Platforms like Fatboy Motorbikes or Motoasia offer monthly contracts with insurance included. Pricier per month, but the peace of mind is worth it.

Golden rule: Before you ride off, take photos and a video of every scratch, every dent, every imperfection. Send them to the rental shop via LINE with the date. That's your only protection against the classic "damage" scam when you return the bike. I watched a tourist get charged 8,000 baht for a scratch that was already there.

The license: what you actually need

Let's be clear: legally, you need either an international driving permit (category A / motorcycle) or a Thai driving license. A standard European car license does not cover two-wheelers, not even a 125cc — despite what you might read on forums. And your home country license on its own, without a translation or an international permit, has zero legal standing here.

In practice? 90% of the expats I know rode without one during their first few months. That's not advice, it's an observation. But here's what happens when things go wrong: if you have an accident, your travel insurance won't cover a thing if you don't have the right license. A mate of mine, Julien, hit a manhole cover in Ari back in 2021. Fractured wrist, 45,000 baht in medical bills. His insurance refused to pay out. Forty-five thousand baht out of pocket because he didn't have a piece of paper that costs about 30 euros from the relevant authority back home.

Getting a Thai license is doable in a single day at the Department of Land Transport (DLT) in Chatuchak. You'll need: your passport, a medical certificate (any clinic, 100–200 baht), your home country license translated by your embassy, and patience. You take a color perception test, a reaction time test, and watch a one-hour video. No actual riding test for holders of a foreign license.

Zones to avoid (and ones where it's manageable)

Bangkok isn't one uniform block of chaos. Some areas are perfectly fine on a scooter; others are assisted suicide.

Absolute no-go zones on a scooter:

  • Ratchaprasong / Siam / Chidlom: the intersection in front of Central World is a death trap. Six lanes, buses, taxis, pedestrians everywhere, and zero logic to the lane changes.
  • Sathorn at 8 a.m. and 6 p.m.: it's a parking lot, not a road. You'll put your foot down 400 times over 2 km.
  • The elevated expressways: they're off-limits to anything under 150cc anyway, but some people still try. Don't be that person.
  • Yaowarat (Chinatown) at night: narrow alleys, street food stalls spilling onto the road, tourists crossing without looking. Beautiful on foot, absolute hell on a scooter.

Reasonably rideable zones:

  • The residential sois in Lat Phrao, Ramkhamhaeng, Bang Na: moderate traffic, wide roads — this is where a scooter truly makes sense.
  • Ari / Pradipat: a fairly chill neighborhood, well-organized streets, lots of two-wheelers already.
  • Along the klongs: some of the roads running alongside the canals are magical shortcuts with barely any traffic.

Local tip: Google Maps is often behind on one-way streets and road closures in Bangkok. Use it for the overall route, but trust the signs on the ground. And if you see a line of motorbikes turning somewhere, follow them — they know the shortcuts better than any app.

The police: what they actually check

There are police checkpoints in Bangkok. Not everywhere, not all the time, but they exist. Based on my experience — and that of every expat I hang out with — here's what actually happens:

The cops set up at strategic intersections, often at the beginning of the month (quotas, apparently). Their number one target is riders without helmets. It's the easiest infraction to spot and the easiest to ticket: 400 baht fine. You pay on the spot, they hand you a receipt.

If they do pull you over, they'll ask for your driving license. No license = 1,000 baht fine. They may also check the vehicle's annual road tax (Phor Ror Bor 4) and the mandatory insurance (Phor Ror Bor). If the scooter is rented and those documents aren't up to date, you're the one who pays, not the rental shop.

What they rarely check: engine size, vehicle condition, where you're heading. What they care about is the helmet, the license, and the scooter's paperwork. That's it.

Is there corruption? I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Twice in seven years, an officer has "offered" to settle the fine informally for a lower amount. I'm not recommending or condemning — just stating what happened. But know that you always have the right to ask for an official receipt and pay at the police station.

Gear and daily survival

A helmet is mandatory, and it's the only thing standing between you and a traumatic brain injury. The plastic bowl the rental shop hands you for 200 baht protects against absolutely nothing. Invest in a proper helmet: an INDEX or a REAL in full-face or open-face goes for 800–1,500 baht at any motorcycle accessories shop. It's worth every baht.

Other practical tips I live by every single day:

  • Wear closed-toe shoes. I know, it's 35 degrees. But I've seen what an exhaust pipe does to an ankle in flip-flops. It's not pretty.
  • Keep your headlights on, even in broad daylight. It makes you more visible in the surrounding chaos.
  • Assume nobody has seen you. Not the taxi, not the pickup truck, not the grandma crossing the road. Ride defensively, all the time.
  • Avoid riding after midnight on Friday and Saturday nights. Drunk drivers are a real problem.
  • Check the tires. Rental scooters often have bald tires. In the rain — and it rains a lot in Bangkok — you're basically on an ice rink.

Insurance: the topic everyone ignores

Every vehicle in Thailand must carry mandatory insurance (Phor Ror Bor), but it covers a maximum of 30,000 baht in medical expenses for third parties. For you personally, it covers almost nothing serious.

If you ride regularly, get a voluntary insurance policy (class 1 or class 3) from a Thai insurer — Viriyah, Bangkok Insurance, Dhipaya. Expect to pay 3,000 to 6,000 baht per year depending on the scooter and the coverage. It covers your medical expenses, vehicle damage, and your civil liability.

And make sure your international health insurance (if you have one) actually covers riding a two-wheeler with the correct license. Many policies have specific exclusions on this point. Read the fine print. Seriously.

What I wish I'd known

When I climbed onto that Honda Click in 2018, I wish someone had told me all of this straight. Not the Instagram-grade advice like "rent a scoot bro, it's total freedom." The real version:
