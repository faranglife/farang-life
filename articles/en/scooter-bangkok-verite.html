Riding a Scooter in Bangkok: What Nobody Actually Tells You

This isn't a how-to guide. It's my honest take after 7 years navigating Bangkok on two wheels — the real risks, the myths, and why I kept riding anyway.

Marc· Published 4 February 2023· Updated January 2025· 10 min read

The day I realized I wasn't invincible

September 2019, Rama IV, 6:30 PM, rush hour. I was lane-splitting through a wall of stopped cars between Klong Toei and Lumphini. A taxi swung open its passenger door without warning. I hit the brakes, the front wheel slid on a wet drain cover, and the next thing I knew I was on the ground with my Honda Click 125 pinning my left leg. Nothing broken, but a bloody knee, a ripped-off mirror, and a serious reality check. The taxi driver looked at me, shrugged, and drove off.

I'd been riding in Bangkok for two years at that point. I'd gotten confident. Too confident. That's exactly the trap. And that's why I'm writing this — not to give you some "10 tips for riding a scooter in Thailand" listicle, but to tell you what seven years in the saddle have actually taught me.

The numbers everyone prefers to ignore

Thailand consistently ranks in the global top 10 for road fatalities, sometimes even the top 3 depending on which WHO report you look at. In 2023, roughly 17,000 people died on the country's roads. The majority involved motorbikes. These aren't abstract statistics — they're people you share the road with every single day.

In Bangkok specifically, the sheer density of traffic keeps average speeds down, which somewhat limits the severity of crashes. But "somewhat" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence. Injuries are still common, and local hospitals like Chula and BNH see their daily parade of farangs in shorts with shredded knees.

One detail nobody mentions: Your international health insurance almost certainly excludes scooter accidents if you don't hold a valid motorcycle license from your home country AND a Thai driving license. Check your policy's fine print before you get on, not after. A friend of mine got hit with ฿380,000 in bills at Bumrungrad after a crash. His insurer covered exactly zero of it.

The "everyone does it" myth

This is the argument I see most often in expat Facebook groups. "Thai people ride without a license from the age of 14 and they're fine." No. They're not all fine. You're looking at a massive survivorship bias. The ones who had serious accidents aren't the ones posting sunset photos of their Scoopy in front of a temple.

The reality is that Thai riders who've been navigating traffic since their teens have developed an instinctive reading of the road that you simply don't have. They anticipate the pickup that's about to turn without signaling. They know the dog sleeping outside the 7-Eleven is going to stand up at the worst possible moment. They know every treacherous drain cover in their soi. You don't. And neither did I for my first two years.

What's actually dangerous (and what's not as bad as you think)

After seven years, I have a pretty clear picture of what genuinely poses a threat versus what's just nervous-tourist fantasy. Here's my personal breakdown:

Perceived danger Actual danger (my take) Comment
Dense downtown traffic Moderate Low speeds, rarely above 30 km/h. Damage is limited if you're wearing a proper helmet.
Major roads (Rama IV, Petchaburi, Ratchadaphisek) High Higher speeds, buses and trucks, aggressive lane changes. This is where serious accidents happen.
Riding at night Very high Poor visibility, drunk drivers (especially on weekends), uneven street lighting. I avoid riding after 11 PM as much as possible.
Rain High The real problem isn't the rain itself — it's the oil that rises to the surface of the asphalt in the first 10 minutes. After that it gets better.
Other scooters High Grab riders, Food Panda and Lineman delivery drivers staring at their phones — they're my number one daily threat.
Stray dogs Moderate to high Unpredictable. I've swerved to avoid dozens. One bit my ankle in 2021 near On Nut. Get your rabies shots.
Police Low Regular checkpoints, especially around Asoke and Thong Lo. ฿500 fine if you don't have a license. Rarely more than that.

Why I kept riding anyway

If it's so dangerous, why didn't I sell my scooter and take the BTS like everyone else? Because Bangkok on a scooter and Bangkok by public transport are two completely different cities.

On my scooter, I do Ekkamai to Silom in 20 minutes, weaving against the flow of traffic. In a taxi, that's 50 minutes minimum, sometimes over an hour. The BTS is fast but it doesn't go everywhere, and the last train is at midnight. On my scooter, I know shortcuts through sois that Google Maps doesn't even suggest. I've discovered entire neighborhoods — Phra Khanong, Bang Na, the little streets behind Charoen Krung — simply because I could get lost easily and find my way out just as easily.

There's also the money side of things. My Honda Click cost me ฿38,000 secondhand in 2018. Maintenance runs about ฿2,000 a year. Gas is ฿60 to ฿80 a week. Compare that to ฿200 a day on Grab just to get to work and back, and the math speaks for itself.

And then there's something harder to put a number on: freedom. After seven years, Bangkok is my city. I know it intimately, physically, because I cross it every day out in the open air. Not from behind the tinted window of an air-conditioned taxi. It's a choice, and it's a privilege, but it's also a risk I've consciously accepted.

The rules I set for myself (and never bend on)

I'm not a daredevil. I'm 39, I work remotely, I have a girlfriend waiting for me at home. I have zero interest in ending up in a hospital bed to save three minutes. So here are my non-negotiable rules:

1. Full-face helmet, always. Not some ฿200 plastic half-shell from the market. A real full-face helmet. I ride with an INDEX Titan that cost me ฿1,500 from a seller on Lazada. It's not a Shoei, but it protects my jaw.

2. No alcohol. Zero. Not even two beers. Not even "just to get home." It's Grab or nothing. This rule has probably saved my life more than once without me knowing it.

3. No riding in heavy rain. I pull over under a bridge or in front of a 7-Eleven. I wait 15 minutes. During rainy season, the downpours are intense but short.

4. Valid license. I got my Thai license at the DLT office in Chatuchak. It takes a day, it's not complicated, and it changes everything if you have an accident — for insurance and for the police.

5. I never overtake on the left on major roads. It's tempting. It's also the fastest way to end up under a truck.

Getting your license at the DLT: Bring your passport, a medical certificate (any clinic, ฿100), your French license + sworn translation (from the embassy or a certified translator). You'll do a color perception test, a reaction time test, and a 50-question multiple-choice exam (available in English). Allow half a day at the Chatuchak or Bangkapi office. The license costs ฿205 and is valid for two years the first time, five years upon renewal.

What I wish I'd known from the start

If I could go back in time and talk to the Marc of 2017 who'd just landed in Bangkok with his suitcases and zero experience of Asian traffic, here's what I'd tell him:

Don't start on a scooter right away. Take the BTS, the MRT, the boats, Grab for at least two or three months. Watch the traffic. Learn the logic — because there is one, even if it looks like chaos. Thai people don't drive "any old how." They follow an unwritten code based on vehicle size, flow, and conflict avoidance. When you start anticipating other people's moves without thinking about it, you're ready.

Then start in the small sois of your neighborhood. Not Sukhumvit. Not Rama IV. The quiet little streets where you can make a mistake without consequences. Gradually work your way up. After six months, you'll have developed a sixth sense — or you'll have decided it's not for you, and that's a perfectly respectable decision.

And above all, never let anyone tell you "it's not that dangerous" or "you just have to be careful." It is dangerous. You can be careful and still get taken out. The goal is to reduce the risks as much as possible, not to eliminate them. If you can accept that, and you put your own rules in place, then riding a scooter in Bangkok can become one of the great pleasures of expat life. But with your eyes open. Always with your eyes open.

Seven years, one minor accident, two serious scares, zero regrets. But I knock on wood every morning when I turn the key.
